    How to read this
    • Upside is the headline, but the scenario range matters more. If Bull and Bear differ by more than 100% (say $400 vs $150), the DCF is highly uncertain: treat the weighted value as a midpoint guess, not a target.
    • Margin of safety = (intrinsic − current) ÷ intrinsic. Value investors want a large one because the intrinsic estimate itself can easily be wrong by that much. Below 0% means the price is above even this model's estimate.
    • The sensitivity heatmap shows which assumption the DCF rests on. If the corners are far apart in price, a small change in discount rate or terminal growth flips the verdict: a fragile DCF. Similar colors across the grid mean a sturdier one.
    • If the "DCF reliability low" warning shows, the free-cash-flow history is erratic, and the Peers tab's multiples are a better guide than this absolute number.
    • What it can't tell you: a DCF is only as good as its assumptions. With this app's fixed growth and discount-rate assumptions, fast-growing companies often look badly overvalued. This valuation has never been tested point-in-time; that test comes with SEC filings in Roadmap Phase 3.

      How to read this
      • The overall risk label condenses volatility, tail losses and drawdowns into Low / Moderate / High / Extreme. It describes how bumpy the ride has been, not which direction the stock will go.
      • Beta vs SPY: above 1.3 means market moves are amplified both ways; 0.8 to 1.3 is market-like; below 0.8 is more defensive. The Model Board's Resilience card shows how much the stock actually fell in past crashes.
      • Stress scenarios estimate how the stock would fare in a repeat of past crises. Where the stock existed they use its real history; otherwise they scale the market's drop by beta, a rough approximation.
      • Kelly sizing is a formula for how much to bet given a win rate and a payoff. It's very sensitive to inputs that are never known exactly, which is why practitioners use a fraction of it (such as Half-Kelly). It's shown here to teach the idea, not as a sizing instruction.
      • Macro correlations: a correlation with SPY above 0.7 means most of the stock's moves are market moves, so owning it is partly just owning the market. A strong negative correlation with TLT (bonds) means it tends to fall when rates rise.
      • What it can't tell you: every number here comes from the past. Measures like VaR tend to understate losses in a crisis; the Crisis tab shows how often they were breached.

      How to read this
      • The relative-value score is the headline: 65 or above looks cheap or strong versus peers, 35 or below looks expensive or weak, and between is fair. Always ask why: cheap stocks are often cheap for a reason.
      • For fast-growing companies, revenue growth and gross margin matter most. A premium multiple can be justified when growth and margins lead the group.
      • For mature companies, P/E and EV/EBITDA matter most. The cheapest multiple with stable margins is the classic value signal.
      • The value-trap test: a stock that is cheap on multiples and falling behind on growth and margins is often cheap because it's declining.
      • What it can't tell you: the result depends heavily on who counts as a peer, and like the DCF it hasn't been tested point-in-time yet.

      How to read this
      • The sentiment score runs from -100 to +100, time-weighted with a 48-hour half-life. Above +15 is a positive news flow, -15 to +15 is noise, below -15 is negative.
      • News vs. price alignment compares the mood with the last 20 days of price moves. Bad news with a rising price means the market is shrugging the news off; good news with a falling price means it's ignoring it. Traders read these patterns in different ways, and neither has been tested here.
      • Fewer than 5 headlines means thin coverage, and the score is noisier.
      • The trend chart says more than the snapshot. +30 today after -20 last week is a changing story; +30 for a month may already be reflected in the price.
      • What it can't tell you: the mood score uses VADER, a tool built for social media that misreads financial language, and headline mood has never been tested here as a predictor. The archive needed to test it only began in September 2026.

            Learn

            Every concept this app uses, in three registers: what it is, the intuition, and — the part textbooks skip — what it's bad at. No ticker needed.

            Quick check: three questions

            The "Big Three" financial-literacy questions by Annamaria Lusardi and Olivia Mitchell (GFLEC), used in surveys around the world. Your answers are recorded anonymously as a score only, so we can see whether people learn here.

            1. Suppose you had $100 in a savings account and the interest rate was 2% per year. After 5 years, how much do you think you would have in the account if you left the money to grow?
            2. Imagine that the interest rate on your savings account was 1% per year and inflation was 2% per year. After 1 year, how much would you be able to buy with the money in this account?
            3. True or false? "Buying a single company stock usually provides a safer return than a stock mutual fund."
